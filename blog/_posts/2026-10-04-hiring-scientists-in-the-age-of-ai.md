*An opportunity to put scientific values into practice: a proposal to the research community.*

## Science at an AI-induced crossroads

AI makes papers, proposals and CVs easier to produce, exposing the gap between the outputs we count and the qualities we value. Those proxies were imperfect long before AI.

We can keep patching the system to preserve familiar measures of achievement. Or we can use this disruption to design hiring and funding that better put our scientific values into practice.

This post takes the second path: changing how we distribute scientific opportunity.

## What biomedical discovery needs from scientists

Our focus is **biomedical discovery science**. Translation has a clearer ultimate goal: improving patient outcomes. Conceivably, a few decades from now, much of it could operate as a gigantic reinforcement-learning loop, with human input on goals and consequential decisions.

Discovery can mean uncovering biological mechanisms, developing technologies or constructing theoretical frameworks. It has no comparably settled objective: these pursuits supply directions, but no ready-made ranking of questions. If AI can pursue many problems, we expect **human attention, taste and judgement** to guide which ones deserve effort. An AI-generated discovery can be profoundly valuable; abundant production alone cannot decide what we find worth understanding.

As AI reliably takes over particular tasks, we expect a shift from **doing to orchestrating science**: framing investigations, delegating tasks, coordinating collaborators, and checking and integrating results. We expect this shift to matter most for computational and analytical (dry) work in the near term, with experimental (wet) work following more gradually as laboratory automation develops. Deep scientific understanding remains essential to deciding what to trust and when to change direction. Selection should increasingly emphasise three capabilities:

- **Choosing worthwhile questions:** curiosity, taste, independence and courage.
- **Deciding what to believe and pursue:** judgement, honesty, humility and adaptability.
- **Building knowledge others can trust and use:** generosity, trustworthiness and responsibility.

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Evidence behind the key claims</summary>

**AI can already take over parts of experimental execution.** [Volk et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-37139-y) demonstrated AlphaFlow, which used reinforcement learning to select and evaluate steps in nanoparticle synthesis. Some reward formulations favoured changes that damaged the material, so the researchers redesigned them. This is evidence of autonomous experimentation within a bounded task, and of the judgement still involved in specifying that task.

**Information abundance makes attention allocation more important.** [Herbert Simon (1971)](https://gwern.net/doc/design/1971-simon.pdf) argued that information consumes its recipient's limited attention. Filtering and condensing it therefore matter alongside production. This supplies the economic reasoning for expecting scientific taste to become more important as AI expands possible investigations. That extension to discovery remains a hypothesis.

**Producing equivalent material does not settle what people value.** Synthetic diamonds have [essentially the same chemical and physical properties](https://4cs.gia.edu/interactive-4cs/more-diamond-info/synthetics.html) as natural diamonds. Yet [Newman and Bloom's experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026035) on hypothetical duplicates, especially artworks, show that history and circumstances of creation can also affect valuation. The analogy is that abundance leaves human interest unresolved. It gives no reason to prefer a human-produced finding over an equally sound AI-generated one.

**A predictive success can open an explanatory question.** Suppose a model predicts cellular responses accurately except in one condition. Should we improve its overall score or investigate whether the failure reveals an unrecognised biological state? In this hypothetical example, curiosity notices the anomaly, taste identifies a potentially important question, and judgement tests whether the evidence warrants pursuing it. Technical performance alone cannot choose the scientific direction.

**Producing more can coexist with understanding less.** [Messeri and Crockett (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0) analyse how AI tools can foster illusions of understanding: researchers may overestimate their grasp of a result or mistake exploration of tool-compatible questions for broad inquiry. Their article is a theoretical perspective, rather than an experiment measuring these effects. It gives a reason to retain scrutiny of assumptions, intellectual humility and openness to alternative questions alongside the ability to produce results.

</details>

These capabilities connect human interest to credible discovery. We next need to decide how to recognise and reward them.

## The distribution problem

Recognising these qualities requires context, interpretation and human attention. Judgements are partly subjective, yet evaluators' time, positions and funding are scarce. We must decide who receives close consideration.

High-impact-factor journals and laborious applications have served as filters. We think they poorly operationalise our values, and expect AI to weaken them further as polished applications become cheaper.

**We propose making applications easier for humans to review, rather than handing selection to machines.** Machines can verify and summarise a researcher's record; people should judge it. Scoring contributions automatically would reward registering more, riding on others' outputs and withholding credit, rather than the qualities we value.

**For funding calls, applicants review each other.** Each applicant assesses a few applications from outside their own pool, so marking down a direct competitor cannot help them. This effort replaces panel work, exercises generosity and judgement, and shows how applicants reason. Reviewers decide whether each application is fundable; a lottery then allocates awards among the fundable.

**For jobs, a panel longlists independently.** Applications are confidential, and a single post offers no pool of non-competitors. Panel members and an independent assessor judge each candidate against declared criteria before any discussion. Longlisted candidates take part in structured interviews with a work sample. A lottery serves at most as a tie-break for interview places.

In both tracks, the decisive effort moves from persuasive writing, which AI makes cheap, to reviewing, reasoning and conversation. Both rely on human judgement, whose limitations we return to below.

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Evidence for changing how we allocate attention</summary>

**Assessment reform already recognises broader contributions.** [DORA](https://sfdora.org/read/) rejects journal impact factors as proxies for individual contributions. The [2022 CoARA agreement](https://www.coara.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2022_07_19_rra_agreement_final.pdf-3.pdf) calls for diverse contributions to be recognised through primarily qualitative judgement, supported by responsible metrics. Keeping machines to verifying and summarising evidence follows that principle.

**Application effort consumes substantial research time.** [Herbert et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2013-002800) surveyed 285 researchers about 632 proposals in Australia's 2012 NHMRC funding round. They estimated 34 working days of researcher time per proposal across the team and did not detect an association between preparation time and funding success. This establishes substantial burden in one scheme; it does not establish how much application material evaluators need. A replacement process should be tested for decision quality and total effort across applicants, referees and evaluators.

**Fine distinctions between strong applications are unreliable.** [Pier et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1714379115) replicated the NIH peer-review process and found no agreement among reviewers evaluating the same application. This supports asking reviewers whether an application is fundable rather than how it ranks against others, and letting chance decide among the fundable.

**Random allocation already has operational precedents.** New Zealand's [2026 Explorer Grants](https://gateway.hrc.govt.nz/funding/downloads/2026_Explorer_Grant_Application_Guidelines.pdf) establish fundability through human assessment, then randomly order applications for funding; our funding track follows the same sequence. Surveying earlier Explorer Grant applicants, [Liu et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-019-0089-z) found that 63% considered the lottery acceptable, many on condition that applications were first judged fundable. The fundability judgement matters for legitimacy as well as quality.

</details>

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Can applicants review each other?</summary>

**Distributed peer review already allocates real resources.** [ALMA](https://almascience.eso.org/proposing/alma-proposal-review), the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array, has reviewed all proposals except its Large Programs this way since its 2022 cycle. In an earlier ESO experiment, [Kerzendorf et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-020-1038-y) found distributed review of 172 proposals statistically similar to panel review, although that outcome did not affect allocation. These are telescope proposals, not biomedical fellowships.

**Reviewing outside one's own pool removes the incentive to mark down competitors.** In impartial selection mechanisms such as the exact dollar partition of [Aziz et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2019.06.004), applicants review only outside their own cluster, so their reports cannot affect their own chances. In a simulation with truthful reviewers, impartiality cost about one top candidate in thirty. The theory assumes a single round and fixed pools; it does not address favouritism.

**Reviews should be judged on quality, not agreement.** [Merrifield and Saari (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-4004.2009.50416.x) proposed rewarding applicants whose rankings agree with the consensus, and themselves warned of conservative mediocrity. Rewarding agreement penalises honest disagreement. A sample of reviews should instead be checked for specificity and accuracy.

**AI can draft reviews too.** An AI-assisted review can still help the applicant being reviewed, but it says less about the reviewer's judgement. Judgement should therefore also be assessed in conversation.

</details>

## Make applications easier for humans to review

Human attention is the scarce resource. A CV turns a researcher's work into a persuasive narrative; reviewers then spend their attention decoding it or fall back on journal names and famous laboratories. An application should instead show the work, the person's responsibility for it and observations others can check. Papers belong alongside datasets, protocols, reagents, software and the maintenance that makes them useful.

**Each application combines a short question statement with a verified contribution record.** The statement explains why a question deserves attention, which alternatives were set aside and what evidence would change the applicant's mind. The record lists outputs with persistent identifiers, such as a DOI, database accession or RRID, together with the applicant's role and supporting observations: **independent reuse, inspectability, sustained stewardship and attributable contributions**. Entries count only when a third party certifies them, as ORCID already does for peer review, where only trusted organisations such as publishers can add records. No one observation establishes scientific quality.

**Machines prepare the evidence; they never score or rank.** They can verify entries, summarise what the record shows, and flag inconsistencies, missing information and contributions reviewers tend to overlook, such as negative findings or technical work. Reviewers read the anonymised question statement and record a first judgement before seeing the record and summary; later changes are logged.

The infrastructure should make these records a by-product of doing and sharing research. Repositories, institutions and collaborators can supply versions, roles and supporting observations. Applicants should be able to inspect and correct the record, independently of their PI, and reuse it across applications. A claim copied between services remains one claim.

This makes contributions visible without turning them into a currency. Maintaining a resource, documenting a negative finding or enabling someone else's experiment become visible to reviewers, while registering more, riding on others' outputs or withholding credit from competitors gains nothing.

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Which contributions become observable?</summary>

**A record should start with what we value.** The [INORMS SCOPE framework (2021)](https://inorms.net/scope-framework-for-research-evaluation/) starts from values, probes for unintended consequences and evaluates the evaluation itself. Here, retain the research object, candidate's role, supporting source and dates. Fix what is recorded for each role and field before selection. The following observations describe aspects of contribution, not the scientist as a whole.

| Observation | What reviewers see | Principal blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent reuse | Distinct groups with documented, substantive use of a dataset, protocol, reagent or tool. | Visibility, field size and time since release influence uptake. |
| Inspectability | The materials needed to examine a selected claim are accessible under appropriate conditions and documented sufficiently for specified checks. | Accessible materials and successful technical checks do not establish a correct scientific conclusion. |
| Sustained stewardship | A dated record of responsibility for maintaining a resource, supported by releases, resolved problems or corroborating users. | Quiet preventive work may leave little visible activity. |
| Attributable contribution | Specific responsibilities supported by project records and collaborators, including experimental and technical roles. | Statements can be inflated, and power affects who receives credit. |

**Inspectability depends on the claim being made.** For an analysis, checks might establish that the stated data version, code and model settings are available and that a specified workflow runs. For an experiment, they might establish that protocols, relevant controls and raw measurements can be examined. These are conditions for scrutiny, not substitutes for it. Sensitive materials may require controlled access, while confidential work needs permitted corroboration rather than a demand for public disclosure.

**Traceable actions provide stronger evidence than activity counts.** Retain evidence of actual protocol adoption or tool dependence, not just citations, downloads or commits. Preserve versions and archive dates: these strengthen temporal evidence but prove neither correctness nor authorship. Each reuse link should retain its supporting observation so that reviewers can check it.

**Negative findings deserve credit for the uncertainty they resolve.** In a negative perturbation experiment, controls must establish that the intervention and measurement worked. The estimate's uncertainty then determines which effects remain plausible. A precise null result and an inconclusive study can both be worth sharing, but support different conclusions. Reuse may miss their value when they prevent work that never happens. The record should preserve the evidence and the candidate's responsibility so that a human can judge what was learned.

**Responses to scientific errors need interpretation rather than a tally.** Several corrections could reflect careless work, close scrutiny or openness about mistakes. Ask what happened after a substantiated problem was identified: was its scope explained, were affected materials updated, and were users informed? Neither many corrections nor their absence establishes honesty. Retain the account for human evaluation. An allegation or automated flag must remain distinct from an established finding, with a chance to respond before an adverse judgement.

</details>

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Existing infrastructure can supply the record</summary>

**Existing infrastructure can connect outputs, people and their contributions.** [Crossref relationships](https://www.crossref.org/documentation/schema-library/markup-guide-metadata-segments/relationships) link research objects; [ORCID](https://info.orcid.org/ufaqs/what-does-source-mean-in-an-orcid-record/) records sources of assertions; [CRediT](https://credit.niso.org/contributor-roles/) supplies a vocabulary for responsibilities. The [FAIR principles](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18) describe conditions for finding, accessing and reusing those objects. A deposited relationship remains an assertion, not independent confirmation of substantive use.

**Different relationships carry different evidence.** Distinguish voluntary adoption from required use of facility tools: both can be useful, but mandated use alone is weak evidence of independent endorsement. Later collaboration should not erase earlier independent uptake.

**Contribution records should include the people author order obscures.** Technicians, software engineers and curators may make discoveries possible through reliable assays, maintained tools and interpretable data. Record their responsibilities without assigning fixed numerical shares of a paper's value. Within-team use remains evidence of usefulness, even where it supplies little evidence of independent uptake. Provide confidential corrections and seek corroboration from contributors chosen by evaluators as well as applicants; dependent colleagues may be unable to challenge inflated credit publicly.

**The institutions holding information should help supply it.** Repositories can expose deposits and versions; institutions can provide employment context; project records can capture responsibilities. Researchers should confirm and correct details without rewriting them for every call. Fund these services and count record maintenance and human scrutiny in the evaluation's total cost. Moving application effort into bookkeeping would not reduce the burden.

</details>

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Reading order protects judgement from anchoring</summary>

**Known anchors still move expert judgements.** In [Englich, Mussweiler and Strack (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167205282152), legal professionals' sentencing decisions moved towards anchors they knew had been set by rolling dice. A summary read first could anchor reviewers in the same way.

**Committing to a view first changes how advice is used.** In a study of 19 veterinary radiologists, [Fogliato et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3531146.3533193) found that those who recorded a provisional decision before seeing an AI's output agreed with it less often, whether it was right or wrong, without becoming more accurate. A first judgement protects independence; it does not by itself improve accuracy.

**Names and affiliations sway reviewers.** In a conference experiment, single-blind reviewers favoured papers from famous authors and top institutions ([Tomkins, Zhang and Heavlin, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1707323114)). [Huber et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2205779119) found reviewers far less likely to recommend rejecting a paper when a Nobel laureate rather than a junior researcher was named as its author. Anonymising the question statement keeps the first judgement on the reasoning; the record necessarily reveals more.

</details>

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Why we don't score contributions</summary>

**Without contribution tracking, scoring cannot separate riding from cooperation.** Undivided credit rewards a minor role on a famous laboratory's or consortium's resource; dividing credit by authors or institutions penalises naming collaborators, technicians and partners. Tracking responsibility for each reused component would resolve this, but the necessary provenance and certification do not exist.

**No measure can both never penalise sharing and never reward size.** If registering an output can never lower a score, a larger portfolio of equally good outputs can only score the same or higher, and with uptake varying between outputs it scores higher on average. Averages avoid rewarding size but penalise registering modest work, null results and corrections; sums and similar measures do the reverse. This follows from the definitions rather than from data.

**Scores invite coordination and drift.** Coordinated web links can manipulate PageRank ([Gyöngyi and Garcia-Molina, 2005](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2005/papers/p517-gyongyi.pdf)), and in Italy, citation patterns consistent with citation clubs rose after careers were tied to bibliometric indicators ([Baccini et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0221212)). In a model, [Smaldino and McElreath (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.160384) show that selecting on measured output can degrade methods even without deliberate gaming.

**Recognition can inform without becoming a currency.** In a meta-analysis of 128 experiments, [Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) found that expected, contingent tangible rewards reduced intrinsic motivation, while informational positive feedback increased it. Laboratory tasks differ from careers, but the distinction supports showing what contributions enabled rather than scoring them.

</details>

## Fund organisations for infrastructure, individuals for science

The shift towards orchestration also changes how scientific work can be organised and funded.

An expert may achieve more alone, while collaboration between capable researchers may make larger investigations possible. This could make the permanent PI-led laboratory less central to scientific organisation. Independently supported researchers could form collaborations around questions without each building a laboratory of their own.

**Fund organisations for infrastructure and individuals for the science.** Organisations sustain facilities, research services, technical staff and training. Individuals receive stable, flexible support to choose questions and pursue them, pooling resources when collaboration helps. Scientific direction should follow emerging evidence, rather than a predefined project or the need to sustain a particular laboratory. Fixed-size individual awards that do not stack remove the incentive to grow a group for its own sake.

Separating scientific support from employment and infrastructure could reduce a PI's control over a researcher's career. Disagreement or departure should not threaten unrelated support. Mentorship and leadership remain valuable, while independence becomes less dependent on one PI's approval. Institutional power still needs accountability.

Both selection tracks should assess scientific judgement and contributions to collective work. Renewal should examine learning and justified changes of direction. Assess organisations on the capacity and access they sustain.

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">How organisations and individuals share responsibility</summary>

**Orchestration changes what an expert can carry from question to result.** A computational scientist might use AI to explore explanations for a cellular response, then collaborate with a cell biologist and an imaging specialist to design and interpret experiments. Shared facilities make this possible without the computational scientist employing a complete laboratory. The collaborators contribute different capabilities and judgement, with explicit responsibility for the final inference.

**AI-assisted individual performance has some experimental support.** In a preregistered experiment with 791 P&G professionals, [Dell'Acqua et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702) found that individuals using AI matched teams without AI on product-innovation tasks. Human judgement retained value in evaluating ideas. This supports the possibility that AI changes how individual and collective capabilities combine. It does not establish the future size of biomedical research teams or show that individual work generally outperforms collaboration.

**Organisations should sustain capabilities that many scientists share.** Fund facilities, computing, repositories, reliable research services and the people who develop and maintain them. Organisations should provide employment continuity and training, rather than leave these dependent on successive PI grants. Access to scarce capacity needs transparent allocation and a route to challenge decisions; otherwise institutional gatekeepers could simply replace laboratory gatekeepers. Review organisational support against reliability, access and the scientific work enabled.

**Individual support should confer discretion over scientific direction.** Provide protected research time and a flexible investigation budget, with access to organisational resources independent of membership in a particular PI's group. Researchers can pool allocations around a joint question, agreeing responsibilities, credit and shared decisions. Larger collaborations may need increased allocations to their participants. This supports collective work without making a permanent hierarchy or a detailed project promise the condition for pursuing it.

**Infrastructure commitments can be specific while discovery remains open-ended.** An organisation building an instrument or maintaining a shared resource needs a budget, delivery plan and accountability to its users. Individuals using that capacity should retain freedom to revise their questions. This is a division of funding responsibilities, not a hierarchy of contributions: developing infrastructure can itself require substantial scientific judgement and deserve individual credit.

**Individual applications and renewal should expose consequential decisions.** Link the contribution record, explain the scientific direction and request support. At renewal, examine selected decisions using existing research records: what was uncertain, what evidence arrived and what changed? An abandoned direction demonstrates judgement when the evidence justified abandoning it. A comprehensive diary or an elaborate prediction of discoveries should not be required.

**Flexible support and shared infrastructure already have working models.** [NIGMS's MIRA](https://www.nigms.nih.gov/Research/mechanisms/MIRA/Pages/faqs-mira-par-22-180) supports broad research programmes rather than fixed specific aims, and expects separately supported investigators to collaborate using their awards. [Arc Institute](https://arcinstitute.org/programs/core-investigators) combines renewable eight-year support with shared Technology Centers and facilities. Both retain PI-led laboratories; our proposal extends independence beyond their leaders. Earlier [observational HHMI comparisons](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-2171.2011.00140.x) found more highly cited papers and publication patterns consistent with exploration, but combined several funding conditions. These precedents support testing flexibility, not a claim that our full funding model is validated.

**Continuity must leave room for new entrants.** [Bol et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1719557115) found that applicants just above an early-career funding threshold subsequently obtained substantially more funding than those just below it, despite similar initial review scores. Protect support within an agreed period, reserve funding for entrants and assess recent contributions at renewal. Organisations should manage employment transitions without making each collaborator's position depend on one individual's renewal.

</details>

Flexible support could let collaborations follow scientific questions. Its benefits depend on how we allocate it.

## What remains unresolved

The proposal leaves unresolved problems of effort, judgement and access.

**Reviewing each other asks a lot of applicants.** Careful reviews take time, and AI-assisted reviews say less about the reviewer. Whether the total burden falls below today's proposal writing needs testing.

**Human interviews can favour extraversion and confident self-presentation.** Structured questions and work samples offer safeguards, but identifying scientific judgement needs validation.

**Reviewers may fall back on prestige.** Anonymised question statements help, but a contribution record reveals where people worked and with whom.

**Lotteries need legitimacy.** Scientists accept them mainly among applications judged comparably fundable, and they rarely suit appointments.

**Visibility and power remain open problems.** Unregistered work stays invisible to the record. Infrastructure providers, certifiers and reviewers may become new gatekeepers. Neither the lottery nor oversight guarantees fair access.

<details class="aside" markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Can human evaluation and oversight earn our trust?</summary>

**Human evaluation needs its own validation.** [Sackett et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000994) found structured interviews among the strongest selection methods in their employment meta-analysis. This supports consistent questions and standards, but does not validate our scientific prompts. Reviewers should record assessments of concrete decisions before seeing the record summary and remain free to disagree with it.

**Conversational confidence can influence interview ratings.** [Wilmot et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000415) link extraversion to higher ratings, especially in less structured interviews; [Peck and Levashina (2017)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00201) link self-promotion and related tactics to higher ratings. These concern general hiring, not biomedical discovery or a comparison of in-person with remote interviews. Whether structured questions and work samples distinguish scientific judgement from presentation remains an empirical question here.

| Capability | Question to explore | Evidence to seek |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choosing worthwhile questions | Why did this question deserve attention? | What its answer could change and which alternatives were set aside. |
| Deciding what to believe and pursue | What did you delegate, and what did you need to check yourself? | Scientific assumptions, discriminating checks and reasons to revise the direction. |
| Building trustworthy knowledge | What did the collaboration make possible, and what was your part? | Complementary contributions, clear responsibility and appropriate credit. |

**Preparation and probing can coexist.** The [Sanger Excellence Fellowship's 2025 guidance](https://www.sanger.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/Excellence-Fellowship-Guidance-and-FAQ-for-Candidates-Jun-2025.pdf) shares competency questions in advance while retaining scientific discussion. Allow preparation, then vary a premise or introduce a plausible AI error and ask what would change the answer. Give time to think. Work samples should reflect relevant decisions, with clear AI rules, support and compensation for substantial work. Request references late to clarify responsibility or behaviour.

**Selection rules need regular independent review.** [DORA, the Global Research Council and Science Europe's 2026 guide](https://sfdora.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/DORA_PracticalGuideRRARFOS_2026_V1.pdf) recommends continual policy review, monitoring and human override in AI-assisted funding. Use spot checks to audit records and machine summaries, review the criteria and test how they change access. Keep rules fixed within rounds; publish revisions beforehand. Independent oversight, recusal and recorded reasons should address the influence of those who design the process and panel patronage. The guide does not validate our particular process.

**Reducing PI dependence can leave other concentrations of power intact.** Technical staff and early-career researchers need secure careers and independent challenges to credit, reference or access decisions without retaliation. Shared infrastructure could worsen dependence if alternatives disappear. Continuity during disputes, agreed handovers and portable support where feasible should make leaving possible without threatening unrelated support. Institutional and reputational power would remain.

**The test is improvement over current practice.** Compare procedures at similar human evaluation capacity, counting effort across applicants, referees, institutions and evaluators. Independently inspect random samples rejected by each process, including candidates rejected by both. Examine errors, access through the lottery and sensitivity to review criteria. Later outcomes cannot establish what rejected candidates would have achieved with support.

**Expansion should depend on scientific freedom in practice.** Test access to facilities, freedom to change collaborators and the ability to challenge decisions and receive credit. Systematic omissions, retaliation or new institutional barriers call for revision. A drift towards easily documented outputs and away from uncertain questions would challenge the design itself.

</details>

The aim is better discovery and greater scientific freedom. A pilot should test the proposal against current practice, revising its criteria and institutions wherever they fail those values.
